## 1. MEWS (the answer key)
Subbe CP, Kruger M, Rutherford P, Gemmel L. Validation of a modified Early Warning Score in medical admissions. QJM 2001;94(10):521-526. https://doi.org/10.1093/qjmed/94.10.521

- Read: abstract on academic.oup.com; text of the article PDF; Table 1 cross-checked against two open-access reproductions (PLOS ONE 2019, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6776326/table/pone.0223155.t001 and https://sjtrem.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13049-015-0194-z/tables/2).
- Table 1 as used in the model:
  - Systolic BP (mmHg): 70 or less = 3; 71 to 80 = 2; 81 to 100 = 1; 101 to 199 = 0; 200 or more = 2
  - Heart rate (bpm): 40 or less = 2; 41 to 50 = 1; 51 to 100 = 0; 101 to 110 = 1; 111 to 129 = 2; 130 or more = 3
  - Respiratory rate (per min): under 9 = 2; 9 to 14 = 0; 15 to 20 = 1; 21 to 29 = 2; 30 or more = 3
  - Temperature (C): under 35 = 2; 35 to 38.4 = 0; 38.5 or more = 2
  - AVPU: Alert = 0; Voice = 1; Pain = 2; Unresponsive = 3
- The published table prints "<70" then "71-80" and "<40" then "41-50". The values 70 and 40 are assigned to the lower band here. No scripted vital sits on those edges.
- "It was decided from previous experience to define a MEWS of five or more as a 'critical score'."
- 709 medical emergency admissions, March 2000, one UK district general hospital. Scores of 5 or more: death OR 5.4 (95% CI 2.8 to 10.7), ICU admission OR 10.9 (2.2 to 55.6), HDU admission OR 3.3 (1.2 to 9.2).
- Oxygen saturation and urine output are not in this MEWS.
- Hospitals use different MEWS variants and different trigger scores. The PLOS ONE table lists a trigger of 4.

## 2. Rapid response calling criteria
Rapid Response Systems. PSNet primer. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. Reviewed 15 September 2024. https://psnet.ahrq.gov/primer/rapid-response-systems

- Read. Table 2, "Typical Rapid Response System Calling Criteria": heart rate over 140 or under 40; respiratory rate over 28 or under 8; systolic BP over 180 or under 90; oxygen saturation under 90% despite supplementation; acute change in mental status; urine output under 50 cc over 4 hours; staff member has significant concern about the patient's condition.
- 2008 Joint Commission National Patient Safety Goal required a way for staff to "directly request additional assistance from a specially trained individual(s)".
- Evidence: the primer says rapid response systems slightly reduce unexpected cardiac arrests on wards and do not change overall hospital mortality.

## 4. Sepsis
- CDC. About Sepsis. https://www.cdc.gov/sepsis/about/index.html. Read: signs include clammy or sweaty skin; confusion or disorientation; extreme pain or discomfort; fever, shivering or feeling very cold; high heart rate or weak pulse; shortness of breath.
- Evans L, et al. Surviving Sepsis Campaign: international guidelines for management of sepsis and septic shock 2021. Crit Care Med 2021;49(11):e1063-e1143. https://sccm.org/sepsisguidelines. Read (SCCM page and guideline text): recommends against qSOFA compared with SIRS, NEWS or MEWS as a single screening tool; names SIRS, qSOFA, SOFA, NEWS and MEWS among tools used for screening; says neither SIRS nor qSOFA is an ideal screening tool and the bedside clinician needs to understand the limitations of each; suggests measuring blood lactate in adults suspected of sepsis; sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies and treatment should begin immediately.

## 5. Nurse concern
Douw G, et al. Nurses' worry or concern and early recognition of deteriorating patients on general wards in acute care hospitals: a systematic review. Critical Care 2015;19:230. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-015-0950-5. Read: 18 studies; 37 signs summarized in 10 indicators; worry or concern can be present with or without a change in vital signs; whether a worry criterion improves outcomes is unclear.

## 7. Skin tone
- Nursing Physical Assessment, College of DuPage (Pressbooks), 6.7 Skin: Inspection. https://cod.pressbooks.pub/nursingphysicalassessment/chapter/skin-inspection/. Read: pallor may look ashen or gray on darker skin and yellowish on brown skin; on lighter skin the skin looks paler and less pink; check mucous membranes, nail beds, conjunctiva and palms.
- Sjoding MW, et al. Racial bias in pulse oximetry measurement. N Engl J Med 2020;383:2477-2478. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7808260/. Read: with a pulse oximeter reading of 92 to 96%, arterial saturation was under 88% in 11.7% of measurements in Black patients and 3.6% in White patients (University of Michigan cohort), and 17.0% and 6.2% (multicenter cohort).

## Added 15 November 2026: who may call rapid response
- AHRQ PSNet primer (read): "Any staff member may call the team if one of the following criteria is met." Table of models: Medical Emergency Team lists physicians and nurses; Rapid Response Team lists a critical care nurse and a respiratory therapist. The 2008 Joint Commission goal required systems for staff "to directly request additional assistance from a specially trained individual(s) when the patient's condition appears to be worsening."
- The Joint Commission, hospital standard NPG.01.05.02 (read in a search extract of the standards document): "The hospital recognizes and responds to changes in a patient's condition." Note: hospitals are not required to create rapid response teams to meet it. EP 1 requires written criteria describing early warning signs and the action to take. https://digitalassets.jointcommission.org/api/public/content/9ca80055182b4274842a5780a94f2c82
- Honarmand K, et al. SCCM guidelines on recognizing and responding to clinical deterioration outside the ICU: 2023. Crit Care Med 2024;52(2):314-330 (statements read on sccm.org): hospital wide RRS with explicit activation criteria, strong recommendation, moderate certainty; patients, families and care partners should be empowered to alert staff including the RRS (good practice statement); no recommendation on who leads the team.
- Chua WL, et al. Int J Qual Health Care 2017;29(8):981-998 (Table 2 read on PMC): one included survey (Bagshaw et al., Canadian teaching hospital, 293 nurses): 75.9% would call the attending physician before activating the team; 15.4% reported reluctance from fear of criticism; 48% would activate for a patient they were worried about with normal vital signs.
- Hecht JD, et al. Worldviews Evid Based Nurs 2024, doi 10.1111/wvn.12700 (abstract extract only): review of nurse led RRT activation in the US; leader and physician support were facilitators and became barriers when absent. Volume and pages not confirmed.
- Not confirmed: the date of the Joint Commission document; full text of Hecht 2024.
